Exploring the limits...

Puzzle: Let the sequence \( \{ x_n: n = 1,2,3,... \} \) be defined by

\( x_n = n \) for \( n = 1,2,3, ... 10 \)

and for \( n > 10 \), each \( x_n \) is the mean of the previous ten terms, i.e.

\( x_n = \frac{1}{10} \sum_{i=1}^{10} x_{n-i} \)

Evaluate: \( \quad \lim\limits_{n \to \infty} x_n \) = ?

Given some positive integer \( k \), we define the sequence \( \{ x_n : n = 1,2,3,... \} \) starting with the numbers from 1 to \( k \), after which each term is the average of the previous \( k \) terms.

That is...

\( x_n = \begin{cases} n & \quad \text{for } n \leq k \\ \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^{k} x_{n-i} & \quad \text{for } n > k \end{cases} \)

Evaluate in terms of k: \( \quad \lim\limits_{n \to \infty} x_n \) = ?

Solution 1: CLICK TO REVEAL

I will start by actually solving the much more general problem:
Given some initial sequence \( \{ x_1 , ... x_k \} \), after which each term is the average of the previous \( k \) terms, what is the limit of \( x_n \) as \( n \) goes to infinity?

On the other hand, I'm not presenting a rigorous proof that the sequence converges! I am confident enough that it does to assume so, and proceed to evaluating the limit. But we can make a few observations. Think of the process of adding terms recursively, with the last \( k \) terms at each step of the way being treated as the current 'state'. So at each step the oldest term in the state (the \( k \)th last term) is replaced by the average of the \( k \) terms in the state. Thus the range of the terms in the state never increases, and (except in the trivial case with all terms equal and no range) the range repeatedly decreases, because the maximum and minimum term always ends up being replaced by a new average term. It should be posible to show that the standard deviation as well as range gets reduced, but a full proof would need just a little bit more, such as a repeated reduction in range or deviation by a particular factor.

  1. By induction, each term of the sequence is a linear combination of the first \( k \) terms.

    \( x_n = \lambda_{n,1} x_1 + ... + \lambda_{n,k} x_k \)

    Starting with for \( n = 1, ... k \)

    \( \lambda_{n,j} = \begin{cases} 1 & \quad \text{for } j = i \\ 0 & \quad \text{otherwise } \end{cases} \)

    And then for \( i > k \)

    \( \lambda_{n,j} = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_{n-i,j} \)

  2. We see that for each \( j \), sequence of \( \{ \lambda_{n,j} \} \) is generated by the same rule as the main sequence, and should converge. Let

    \( \lambda_j = \lim\limits_{n \to \infty} \lambda_{n,j} \)

  3. Also by induction, we can see that for each \( n \), the coefficients \( \{ \lambda_{n,j} : j = 1, ... k \} \) sum to 1. That is, each term is a particular weighted arithmetic mean of the first \( k \) terms. So the limit we seek should also be a weighted mean of the given first \( k \) terms, expressed as:

    \( \lim\limits_{n \to \infty} x_n = \lambda_1 x_1 + ... + \lambda_k x_k \)

  4. The key observation now is that because of the recursive nature of the sequence, any subsequence of \( k \) consecutive terms could be treated as the initial set, and the same weightings (the \( \lambda_j \) ) applied.

    In particular, we can equate using the original first \( k \) terms \( 1, ..., k \) with using the terms \( 2, ..., k+1 \)

    \( \begin{align} \lambda_1 x_1 + ... + \lambda_k x_k &= \lambda_1 x_2 + ... + \lambda_{k-1} x_k + \lambda_k x_{k+1} \\ &= \lambda_1 x_2 + ... + \lambda_{k-1} x_k + \lambda_k \tfrac{1}{k} ( x_1 + ... + x_k ) \end{align} \)

  5. Since this holds for arbitrary selections of \( x_1 , ... x_k \) we can equate the coefficients

    \( \begin{align} \lambda_1 &= \tfrac{1}{k} \lambda_k \\ \lambda_2 &= \lambda_1 + \tfrac{1}{k} \lambda_k \\ \lambda_3 &= \lambda_2 + \tfrac{1}{k} \lambda_k \\ ... \\ \lambda_k &= \lambda_{k-1} + \tfrac{1}{k}\lambda_k \\ \end{align} \)

    Hence, letting \( \lambda = \lambda_1 = \frac{\lambda_k}{k} \) we have

    \( \lambda_j = j \lambda \text{ , so} \\ \\ 1 = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \lambda_j = \tfrac{1}{2} k ( k+1 ) \lambda \\ \\ \lim\limits_{n \to \infty} x_n = \dfrac{ \sum_{j=1}^{k} j x_j }{ \tfrac{1}{2} k ( k+1 ) } \)

    In other words, the final limit mixes the orginal \( k \) numbers in ratio \( 1:2:...:k \).

  6. Applying to the special case where \( x_n = n \text{ for } n = 1, ..., k \) we get

    \( \begin{align} \lim\limits_{n \to \infty} x_n & = \dfrac{ \sum_{j=1}^{k} j^2 }{ \tfrac{1}{2} k ( k+1 ) } \\ \\ & = \dfrac{ \tfrac{1}{6} k ( k+1 )( 2k + 1 ) }{ \tfrac{1}{2} k ( k+1 ) } \\ \\ & = \dfrac{ 2k + 1 }{ 3 } \\ \end{align} \)

  7. For the original question, \( k = 10 \text{, so } \lim x_n = \tfrac{2.10 + 1}{3} = 7 \)
